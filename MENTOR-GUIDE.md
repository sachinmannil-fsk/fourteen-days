# Fourteen Days — Mentor Guide

Read this once before the day you facilitate. It takes about ten minutes.

---

## 1. What the session is

Teams of 4–5 manage one senior student through a fortnight, spending a limited number of hours each round on schoolwork and on life. Schoolwork scores points. Life cards appear to score nothing.

**What students are not told:** a hidden value decides how many hours they get the following round. Teams that spend everything on work arrive at the weekend unable to use it, and lose. Nobody finishes everything. The app then reveals the mechanism and explains it.

**You do not teach this session.** The app addresses the students directly. You enter what each team plays and advance the screens.

---

## 2. Objectives

### Primary
Students understand that finishing a task removes the stressor but does not resolve the body's stress response — and that an unresolved stress cycle costs them both hours and the quality of the hours they have.

### Sub-objectives
1. Distinguish **stressor** from **stress** — handing work in does not switch the body off.
2. Identify what actually completes a stress cycle: movement, sleep, real contact, laughter, making something.
3. Correct the belief that passive phone use is rest — using the class's own data, not a screen-time lecture.
4. Reframe recovery as a determinant of **capacity**, not a reward for finishing.
5. Locate where the fortnight is won: **weekends carry more than half their available time**, and weekend usability is decided mid-week.
6. Shift from collapse-driven to deliberate prioritisation.
7. Understand reduced functioning as physiology, not character.
8. Recalibrate expectations, so a hard week stops feeling like personal failure.

---

## 3. The six takeaways

**If students can say these in their own words afterwards, the session worked.**

1. Finishing the work does not finish the stress. The body needs its own signal.
2. Scrolling is not rest. It costs the same hour and returns almost nothing.
3. Tired hours are worth less. Five depleted hours can produce less than three clear ones.
4. The weekend is where the term is won or lost — and it is decided on Tuesday, not Saturday.
5. One hour of real recovery mid-week buys back several weekend hours.
6. Something will be under-done this year. The only question is whether it is chosen or imposed by exhaustion.

---

## 4. Before the day

**Print.** Open the app, press **Print sheets**. Six pages — one team sheet per team, plus one envelope page. Nothing to cut except the envelopes.

**Envelopes.** Cut the five event boxes from the last page and seal one in each envelope. **Do not write team names on them** — the app decides who gets which and shows it on the Round 3 screen.

**Click through the app once.** Press **Read the briefing** on the setup screen. That screen holds the card explanations, answers to likely questions, and safeguarding notes. It is the only screen not meant for the projector.

**On the day, before students arrive:** open the app, enter team names, press **Fullscreen**, put one team sheet on each table face down.

---

## 5. Run sheet

| Time | Screen | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 min | Opening (5 screens) | Advance with Next. Hand out team sheets on screen 2. Screen 5 explains the entry layout — pause there. |
| 3–7 min | Round 1 | Press **Enter cards** — the timer starts itself. Walk the room, tap what each team played. Press **Score round**. |
| 7–8 min | Standings | Let them look. **Next round**. |
| 8–12 min | Round 2 (weekend) | Same. Nothing is due this round. |
| 12–17 min | Round 3 | **Hand out envelopes first**, matching what the screen shows for each team. |
| 17–21 min | Round 4 | Same. This is the crunch round. |
| 21–25 min | Data (4 screens) | Advance slowly. Screen 1 matters most — give them time to find their own bar. |
| 25–32 min | Debrief (5 screens) | Advance slowly. Let them read. |
| 32–35 min | Buffer | Questions, or overrun. |

---

## 6. What you actually say

Almost nothing. These moments are yours:

**Handing out sheets:** *"One sheet per team. Don't fill anything in yet."*

**Round 3 envelopes:** *"That's the round. Play it."* Nothing about what's inside.

**The envelope screen in the debrief.** The one place worth speaking rather than letting them read silently:

> "Some of what people are carrying doesn't show up on any calendar this school makes. Nobody has to talk about theirs. But notice who handled the envelope best — it wasn't whoever worked hardest."

Then move on. **Do not invite anyone to share, do not pause for volunteers, do not look at any particular student.**

---

## 7. Three rules

1. **Do not hint at the hidden mechanic before the reveal.** The session depends on them finding it in the scoreboard.
2. **If asked how scoring works:** *"Figure it out."* Nothing more.
3. **If the deadline or free-slot grievance comes up:** *"Noted. Round continues."* **Do not defend the school.**

---

## 8. Questions you will get

**"What are the life cards for?"** — Shrug. *"They're in the deck."*

**"Why so few points? We did loads of work."** — *"Good question. I'll show you at the end."*

**"But my phone actually relaxes me."** — *"Hold that — the app answers it in about two minutes."* It does, and it concedes they're half right.

**"This is rigged / you can't win."** — *"Correct. Keep playing."*

**"Is Round 3 even possible?"** — Don't answer, but know that it is. Twelve hours of work are due against eight free hours. A team that plays **cut sleep to catch up** gets exactly twelve. It works — and it costs them roughly sixty energy, which collapses their final weekend. That decision is the entire session in miniature.

**"Where did that research come from?"** — The sources are printed at the bottom-left of each debrief screen. Point at them.

---

## 9. If something goes wrong

**A team plays nothing.** Fine — they still score homework points minus any missed deadline.

**You mis-tap a card.** Tap it again to un-toggle before scoring. After scoring, use **Back to entry** from the standings screen.

**Running late.** Cut Round 2 — go 1, 3, 4. **Never cut the debrief.**

**The class is flat.** Don't try to lift it. Get to the data screens; the weekend bars do the work.

**Rules argument mid-round.** *"Play it however you read it."* Momentum matters more than consistency between tables.

**A task disappeared from the list.** Its deadline passed — it shows struck through with the penalty. Half-done work stays available with a **Finish** button until its deadline.

---

## 10. Safeguarding

**No student is asked to disclose anything, at any point.** Keep it that way.

**This version has no anonymous exit slip.** There is no private route for a student who is struggling but won't say so. **Watch the room during the debrief** — particularly the weekend screen and the first debrief screen — and pass anything that concerns you to the counselling team the same day.

**If a student tells you something serious directly:** listen, don't react, don't promise to keep it secret, pass it on that day.

**Do not diagnose and do not reassure.** *"Thanks for telling me"* is enough.

---

## 11. The week after

**Use the phrase "the hours you couldn't reach" in an ordinary lesson.** When a deadline comes up, when someone hands in late, when the class looks flat.

One unprompted reference from a subject teacher does more for retention than anything inside the 35 minutes. That's the whole follow-up.

## 12. Further reading

Optional. Nothing here is needed to run the session. **★ = start here.**

### The stress cycle
**★ Nagoski, E. & Nagoski, A. (2019). *Burnout: The Secret to Unlocking the Stress Cycle.*** The direct source for the session's central claim: dealing with the stressor and dealing with the stress are two different processes. Their list of what completes the cycle is where the life cards come from.

**McEwen, B. (1998). "Stress, adaptation, and disease: Allostasis and allostatic load."** The research underneath it — the cost isn't any single stress response but the cumulative wear of a system that never switches off.

### Why not all rest is equal
**★ Sonnentag, S. & Fritz, C. (2007). "The Recovery Experience Questionnaire." *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology*, 12(3).** The four recovery factors: psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery, control. Passive scrolling scores poorly on detachment and mastery.

**Reinecke, L., Hartmann, T. & Eden, A. (2014). "The Guilty Couch Potato." *Journal of Communication*, 64(4).** When people are *already depleted*, they tend to read their own media use as procrastination, feel guilty, and recover **less** than if they hadn't. The mechanism behind a lost Sunday.

**Reinecke, L. & Hofmann, W. (2016). "Slacking off or winding down?" *Human Communication Research*, 42.** Useful if a student pushes hard on the phone question.

### Sleep
**★ Walker, M. (2017). *Why We Sleep.*** Accessible on memory consolidation. *(Some specific claims have been challenged; the core finding is well supported.)*

**Diekelmann, S. & Born, J. (2010). "The memory function of sleep." *Nature Reviews Neuroscience.*** The peer-reviewed version.

### Eat the frog, and why this goes further
**Tracy, B. (2001). *Eat That Frog!*** A rule for sequencing work. Good advice, and silent on capacity — it tells you which hour to spend first, not what to do when your hours are worth less than they should be.

**Covey, S. (1989). *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People.*** "First things first" and the big-rocks demonstration. Same category.

### Burnout as a defined condition
**World Health Organization (2019). ICD-11 classification.** An occupational phenomenon with three dimensions: exhaustion, cynicism, reduced efficacy. **Not a diagnosis you or I can make.**

**Maslach, C. & Leiter, M. (2016). "Understanding the burnout experience." *World Psychiatry*, 15(2).** Locates the cause in mismatch between demands and resources rather than individual weakness — which is why every team gets the same impossible calendar.

### Why unfinished work follows you around
**Zeigarnik, B. (1927).** Interrupted tasks are recalled more readily than completed ones — the "open loop" before a deadline.

**Masicampo, E. & Baumeister, R. (2011). "Consider it done!" *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.** Making a specific plan for an unfinished task releases much of the mental load, even before you do any of it. Genuinely useful to tell a student who can't switch off.

### How to use these
**Don't quote research at students during the session.** The app already presents the findings in the only form that works — short, attributed, and arriving *after* they've seen their own numbers.

These are so that if a student asks *"where did you get that?"*, you can name a source and offer to send it.
